Research

My work focuses on two main topics: detecting new exoplanets with precision radial velocities and understanding the formation and evolution of sub-Saturns.

Exoplanet Detection with MaHPS at Wendelstein

A central pillar of my PhD has been using the Manfred Hirt Planet Finder Spectrograph (MaHPS), a fibre-fed échelle spectrograph mounted on the 2.1 m Fraunhofer telescope at the Wendelstein Observatory in the Bavarian Alps. MaHPS delivers radial-velocity precision at the few-m/s level, making it a powerful tool for measuring the masses of transiting planet candidates identified by the TESS space mission. Beyond observations, I also contribute to the development and maintenance of the MaHPS data reduction pipelines and have served as the on-site observer for more than 50 nights.

Using MaHPS, I have confirmed and characterised multiple new exoplanets spanning a range of masses and orbital architectures. These include two sub-Saturns near the edge of the Neptune desert — the observed deficit of intermediate-mass planets on short-period orbits — whose stability against photoevaporation supports the idea that the desert's upper boundary is shaped by migration rather than atmospheric loss (Thomas, Hébrard et al. 2025 ↗). I also confirmed two warm Jupiters on notably eccentric orbits, one of which lies on a tidal circularisation track that will eventually bring it to a hot-Jupiter orbit, providing a snapshot of high-eccentricity migration in action (Thomas, Nielsen et al. 2026 ↗). In addition, I contributed to the long-running radial-velocity survey Search for Giant Planets in M 67 using several other spectrographs (HPF, HARPS, HARPS-North, SOPHIE), which led to the discovery of a warm Jupiter orbiting a turn-off-point star in the cluster and an updated hot-Jupiter occurrence rate that remains elevated compared to field stars (Thomas, Saglia et al. 2024 ↗).

Formation and Evolution of Sub-Saturns

Sub-Saturns — planets with radii of roughly 4–8 R⊕ — occupy a fascinating and poorly understood region of parameter space. Too large to be explained by rocky cores with thin atmospheres alone, yet too small to have undergone the runaway gas accretion that builds Jupiter-mass envelopes, they challenge conventional models of giant planet formation. A major thread of my research asks: what can the internal structures and orbital architectures of sub-Saturns tell us about their formation?

Using the interior structure code GASTLI, I modelled a sample of 28 warm sub-Saturns to derive their envelope mass fractions. The resulting distribution is strikingly bimodal, with a gap that aligns with the theoretical threshold for runaway gas accretion — the point at which the envelope mass becomes comparable to the core mass. Sub-Saturns below this gap appear to be planets whose growth stalled before runaway accretion could begin, while those above it managed to trigger runaway accretion but stopped shortly after, perhaps because their natal disk dispersed. A complementary analysis of the hydrogen-helium mass fraction reveals a second gap at ~30%, corroborating earlier theoretical predictions whose physical origin remains unexplained (Thomas, Nielsen, Acuña et al. 2025 ↗).

Building on this, I investigated the companion architectures of 75 sub-Saturn systems by combining radial-velocity injection-recovery tests with transit completeness maps to measure completeness-corrected companion occurrence rates across the Neptunian landscape. The results reveal a striking gradient: nearby-companion rates rise from ~20% in the desert and ridge to ~78% in the savanna, closely mirroring the known dichotomy between hot and warm Jupiters. This supports an evolutionary connection between close-in sub-Saturns and hot Jupiters, both consistent with high-eccentricity migration clearing nearby planets during inward migration. Savanna sub-Saturns, by contrast, reside in dynamically quiet, multi-planet systems — and an eccentricity decomposition shows this distinction even more cleanly than the period-based landscape classification alone.
